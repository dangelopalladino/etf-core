# Archetype Descriptions — Blind Spots & Failure Modes

Replace the DRAFT fields for `blindSpots` and `failureMode` on each archetype object.

## Compass (CP)

### blindSpots

**The busy trap.**
The Compass mistakes activity for progress. They are running at full output — meetings, side projects, conversations, research — and the motion feels productive. It is not. It is circular. The Compass does not see that busyness is the avoidance strategy, not the building strategy. The harder they push without direction, the more exhausted and further behind they feel, which produces more activity, which produces more exhaustion.

**The readiness myth.**
The Compass believes they will start building once the direction is clear. They do not recognize that clarity comes from doing, not from thinking. They are waiting for a certainty that will not arrive before action. The direction that feels right to a Compass emerges from deliberate movement toward something — not from analysis of all available options.

**The excellence default.**
The Compass applies their execution engine to whatever is in front of them, regardless of alignment. They are good at almost everything they try. Being good at something reads as a signal that it is right. It is not. Competence and Core Code™ alignment are different things. The Compass can spend years performing excellently at work that means nothing to them.

### failureMode

The Compass builds an impressive structure in the wrong direction. Five years post-career, they are successful by every visible metric — income, title, stability — and feel exactly as lost as they did in year one. The busyness that was supposed to produce direction has produced a career. The career does not fit. Starting over at this point feels like a betrayal of the work already invested, so the Compass stays. The hollow feeling becomes permanent.

The secondary failure mode: the Compass never starts. Analysis paralysis produces inaction, inaction produces shame, shame produces more analysis. They are still researching the right move ten years after the career ended.

## Mirage (MI)

### blindSpots

**The performance gap.**
The Mirage cannot feel the difference between performing well and being well. In sport, those two things were aligned — great performance produced real satisfaction. Post-career, the Mirage is performing at the same level but the satisfaction is not arriving. They do not understand why. The answer — that they are performing for someone else's values — is invisible to them because they have never separated their own values from the values they were trained to want.

**The success trap.**
The Mirage's biggest obstacle is that they look fine. Their friends think they are fine. Their family thinks they are fine. Every external signal says they have figured it out. This makes it nearly impossible to ask for help or admit something is wrong. The gap between external success and internal experience is not seen by anyone who might help, because the Mirage's performance ability hides it perfectly.

**The sunk cost.**
The Mirage has invested significantly in the wrong direction — years, relationships, professional identity, sometimes a family's financial stability. Admitting the direction is wrong feels like invalidating everything already sacrificed. They do not see that redirecting is not abandonment. It is correction. The skills built in the wrong direction transfer. Nothing was actually wasted.

### failureMode

The Mirage optimizes. They solve the hollow feeling by going harder in the same direction — a bigger title, more income, a higher-status role. The optimization produces more of the same emptiness. The coping patterns that manage the dissonance — drinking, overwork, emotional numbness, chronic busyness — become structural features of the Mirage's life rather than temporary responses to a solvable problem.

The deepest failure mode: the Mirage never names the feeling. They suppress the internal signal well enough that it stops being conscious. The hollow is just how life feels. They stop expecting anything else.

## Sentinel (SN)

### blindSpots

**The self-sufficiency illusion.**
The Sentinel is capable and self-aware and pushing forward. They interpret needing people as weakness — a misread from sport culture where dependence was a liability and toughness was the standard. They do not see that the team was not a dependency. It was a structural requirement. Without it, even the strongest athlete eventually stalls.

**The productivity disguise.**
The Sentinel is making real progress. The career question is being addressed. The structure is functional. Because the build is visible, the isolation is easy to deprioritize. The Sentinel tells themselves they will deal with the relational side after the career piece is sorted. The relational piece does not wait. It deteriorates while the Sentinel is focused elsewhere.

**The reconnection miss.**
The Sentinel assumes that relationships from sport will stay. They do not. The daily proximity that held those relationships together is gone. Without deliberate maintenance — which requires the Sentinel to initiate, which feels uncomfortable — the locker room friendships fade without anyone choosing to end them. The Sentinel looks up one day and the people are simply not there anymore.

### failureMode

The Sentinel builds something real in complete isolation. The build is solid but unsustainable. Without relational infrastructure, the Sentinel hits a Block List entry — a setback, a rejection, a period of low progress — with no one to talk to and no one to pull them through it. Isolation compounds. The coping mechanisms that fill the relational void — overwork, withdrawal, unhealthy substitutes — become the dominant pattern.

The secondary failure mode: the Sentinel burns out. They were carrying the whole structure alone. When the energy runs out, there is no team to absorb the slack.

## Signal (SG)

### blindSpots

**The performance mask.**
The Signal knows how to perform. They have been performing since they were twelve. In crisis, the performance skill activates automatically — they look functional to everyone around them while the internal system is collapsing. This is not conscious deception. It is a trained reflex. The mask makes it nearly impossible for people who care about them to see the actual state, and it makes it very difficult for the Signal to ask for help without feeling like they are breaking a fundamental rule about who they are.

**The grief misread.**
The Signal is grieving, but they do not call it that. Athletes are not given language for grief unless someone dies. The loss of the career — the identity, the community, the structure, the neurochemical baseline — is a grief event. The Signal experiences the symptoms without the framework to understand them, which produces shame (something is wrong with me) rather than recognition (I am going through a loss).

**The timeline expectation.**
The Signal believes they should be further along. Other athletes seem to be handling it. They are not in acute distress in public. The Signal's internal benchmark — based on sport's clear milestones and measurable progress — does not map onto transition, which is nonlinear and slower than expected. The expectation gap produces shame and suppression of the actual experience.

### failureMode

The Signal never gets support. The performance mask holds long enough that the crisis becomes chronic rather than acute. What was a Signal pattern — acute crash requiring focused intervention — becomes the new baseline. The coping mechanisms that got them through the first year become structural features of their life. The unprocessed grief does not resolve; it resurfaces as depression, relationship breakdown, addiction, or health deterioration years later.

The critical failure mode for Signal is delayed recognition. The pattern that is treatable in year one is significantly harder to address in year five.

## Anchor (AN)

### blindSpots

**The homecoming reframe.**
The Anchor has convinced themselves — and often everyone around them — that returning to sport is the right move. The framing is positive: they love the game, they have something to give back, the opportunity was right. The Anchor does not see that the return may be avoidance wearing the clothes of purpose. The difference between a genuine calling back to sport and a retreat to the familiar is not visible from the outside and is actively resisted from the inside.

**The comfort cost.**
The Anchor is not suffering visibly. They are in a familiar environment with familiar language and familiar status markers. The discomfort of the alternative — being a rookie in an unfamiliar arena — is theoretical rather than concrete. The Anchor weighs concrete comfort against theoretical discomfort and chooses comfort without recognizing it as a choice.

**The clock.**
The Anchor does not see that staying is a decision. Every year in the safe harbor is a year the identity question does not get answered. The skills that transfer to civilian life are atrophying. The confidence to be a beginner somewhere new is eroding. The Anchor believes they can leave when they are ready. They do not see that readiness does not arrive without some movement toward the door.

### failureMode

The Anchor retires twice. The first retirement was from sport as an athlete. The second retirement — from sport-adjacent work — happens ten or fifteen years later, when the sport-adjacent role ends and the Anchor is now in their late thirties or forties, with the same identity question they had at twenty-five and significantly less time and energy to answer it.

The secondary failure mode: the Anchor recognizes the trap but cannot leave. The pull of sport-adjacent identity and community is stronger than the pull of the unfamiliar alternative. They stay indefinitely, with increasing awareness that they are staying.

## Catalyst (CT)

### blindSpots

**The claimed values risk.**
The Catalyst is in motion. They have direction and traction and a build underway. What they do not always see is that the direction may be built on claimed values rather than Core Code™. The thing they are building may be what they believe they should want — admirable, coherent, external-validation-approved — rather than what their engine actually runs on. Because the build is working, the misalignment is not obvious. It shows up later as a ceiling — a point where progress stalls and the Catalyst cannot understand why.

**The optimization trap.**
The Catalyst is already running a good system. The temptation is to optimize the system rather than audit it. Fine-tuning what is already working feels more productive than questioning whether the direction is right. The Catalyst can spend years making their current build faster and better when the deeper question — is this the right build — has not been answered.

**The isolation of success.**
The Catalyst's progress creates distance from other athletes in earlier stages of transition. They do not have the same language. The Catalyst can find themselves without peers — too far from the struggle to connect with athletes in crisis, not yet established enough to mentor from a position of completeness. This relational gap is invisible until it produces loneliness in what should feel like a successful period.

### failureMode

The Catalyst hits the ceiling. The build they constructed — competently, intentionally, with real effort — turns out to have been built on claimed values rather than Core Code™. The signs accumulate: diminishing returns, restlessness despite objective success, a nagging sense that this is still not quite it. The Catalyst, who figured out transition better than most, discovers they solved the wrong problem. Redirecting at this stage requires dismantling real progress, which is more costly and more psychologically difficult than redirecting would have been earlier.

The secondary failure mode: the Catalyst never goes deep. They move through transition efficiently but at surface level, building a functional life without ever doing the identity excavation that would make it a meaningful one.
